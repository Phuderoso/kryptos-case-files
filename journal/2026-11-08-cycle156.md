# Cycle 156 — Two Locks on One Door

Hill has been in the interview room more often than anybody. We tried him alone, with an accomplice in front (case 132), with an accomplice behind (case 142). Tonight's question was the obvious one nobody had asked yet: what if he brought two, one on each side of the door, each keeping his own rhythm.

The trick is that two short keys hide inside one long one. A period-3 key in front and a period-5 key behind look, from the outside, like a single key that repeats every thirty letters, and thirty unknowns against twenty-four witnesses is a case you can't make. But the two-man version only has twelve unknowns plus the matrix. So I didn't treat it as a long key. I fixed the matrix one row at a time, in both little worlds, mod 2 and mod 13, and let every fixed row turn the rest into straight algebra. When a row broke the story, every matrix that started with it went in the bin unopened.

Ten planted jobs, ten confessions. Two of them were crooked on purpose: one with a scratched letter on the copper, one with a lie in the clue. Both failed the strict test and both owned up in tolerant mode, naming the exact block. A Python clerk who counted his own way, guessing the outer key and solving the rest, matched the program on all 1,788 counts.

K4. Behind every one of the 165,065 transpositions, both directions, two numberings: eighteen new period pairs for the 2×2 matrix, 47.5 million configurations, not one consistent. The decoys came up empty too, which is what an exact test with this much margin should do. Directly, 153 pairs across 2×2, 3×3 and 4×4: zero, except four long 2×2 pairs where a handful of keys fit the cribs and read like static, same as the shuffled decks. Allow one bad letter and 131 pairs stay clean. The rest sit inside the null range, and four of them are too loose to read at all. I wrote those down as low power.

The bill: 108 seconds for the main run, about six and a half minutes of heavy work in all. Under budget for once.
