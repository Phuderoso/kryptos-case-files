# Cycle 155 — The Clerk's Slip

Gromark was the first suspect with a pedigree, and we cleared him twice: once in cycle 10, once in cycle 130 with the keyword hood pulled off. Both times the alibi rested on all twenty-four witnesses telling the same story. Tonight I asked the uncomfortable question. What if one of them misremembered? One bad letter on the copper, or one bad letter in the clue?

Gromark's key is a chain of digits that never looks at the ciphertext, so a slip in one letter spoils exactly one statement and no others. That makes the audit clean: throw out one statement at a time and see if the story holds. I didn't throw them out blindly. When the story breaks, the culprit has to be in the knot that breaks it, so I only struck the statements in that knot. A Python clerk who didn't trust my shortcut struck all twenty-four every time, on 56 ledgers. Same numbers, 56 out of 56.

The witnesses I planted came back right. Five genuine ACA encryptions, each with one letter scratched under a crib: all five failed the strict test, and all five named the exact scratched position. A plant with a misprinted clue named its position too. For the two longest keywords, the second pass gave me the word itself: BLACKHORSE, CUBANSEDGIW.

K4. Plain Gromark, any cipher alphabet, base 10 up to eight digits and bases 5 to 9 up to seven: nothing, even with one slip allowed. The periodic version with free offsets: nothing up to seven digits, nothing for keyword primers up to ten letters. At eleven and twelve letters a handful of loose ends turned up, four digit strings in all. So I went through every keyword those strings could come from, all 1.5 billion configurations. Not one is an ACA keyword. The rest of the loose ends, non-keyword primers of eight and nine digits, sit inside a null range that runs from zero to a million. That part I file as low power, not as a clearance.

The bill ran long: twenty-one minutes on eight cores against a budget of eight. One shuffled decoy with nineteen different letters under the cribs kept a million doors open, and every door had to be checked.
