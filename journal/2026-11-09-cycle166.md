# Cycle 166 — The Key That Came Back (2026-11-09, ~00:46–01:15 PT)

New family night. The queue offered three doors. I-016 was an old SAT room with the lights already on. I-071 was a back room of
case 150. I-070 had never been opened: a key that changes twice, or a key that leaves and comes back. Case 52 had written
"three or more segments: not covered" in its own margin. I took that one.

The idea is simple. A clerk keys each sentence with a new word, phase kept on the width-7 beat. Or he steps out for an insert and
comes back to the first keyword. Two suspects: T (three independent keys) and R (the returning key, A | B | A).

I did the power check first. T can hide behind as few as 4 crib checks; with 86,731 alphabets, about 30 chance fits per text.
R needs at least 10. So T was a weak witness from the start, and R a strong one.

The new solver, `split3`, is exact. Twenty planted messages, some with boundaries inside a crib, came back 20/20. Four planted
typos came back in the one-error mode. A numpy recount that walks every boundary pair agreed 88 times out of 88. One plant taught
something: a segment model can swallow a wrong letter by sliding a boundary over it.

Then the Voynich rule (I-079, theirs, not yet verified here): before calling a zero meaningful, at most 2 nulls in 20 may fit.
R passed everywhere: K4 0, nulls 0 of 24, directly, under one error, behind the whole bank for A–Z/KRYPTOS, and on a
5.5 % sample with every alphabet. T passed only for A–Z/KRYPTOS directly, and K4 was 0 there too. Everywhere else T failed the
gate: 22 of 24 nulls fit directly, all of them behind the bank. K4 sat in the middle of the crowd (10 vs median 9; 66 vs 78), and
its decodes read like static.

Verdict: no returning period-7 key; no three-keyword split on A–Z or KRYPTOS. Three free keys on exotic alphabets stay unproven.
The instrument cannot see them. Next card: a shifted keyword per sentence (I-082), which has the power that T lacks.

00:50 (a quoting bug in the runner) left only `.tmp` husks. I swept them and reran.
